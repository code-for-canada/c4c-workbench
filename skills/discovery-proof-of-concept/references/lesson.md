# Test the direction before the design

Brainstorming gives you a few genuinely different ways the service could work. Before you invest in any of them, put rough versions in front of the people they're for.

At this stage you're not asking "can people use this?" You're asking two bigger questions: *are we solving the right problem?* and *which direction is worth pursuing?* Showing people concrete ideas is one of the fastest ways to surface needs that interviews alone miss. People find it much easier to react to something than to describe what they want.

## Keep it rough on purpose

A polished prototype invites comments on colours and button labels. A rough storyboard or a few grey screens invites comments on the idea itself: "That wouldn't work for me, because…" is exactly what you want to hear. Showing several concepts side by side also makes it easier for people to be honest, since they're not judging *your* one idea.

## Listen for the problem, not just the preference

The most valuable thing you'll hear is often not which concept someone preferred, but what each one reminded them of: the time a form got lost, the workaround their team uses, the person who always gets left out. That feeds back into your research and sharpens the problem before you commit to a solution.

## What happens next

You come out of this with one direction (or a combination), the evidence for it, and a list of what you still don't know. Alpha picks up from there: refining the chosen direction, testing whether people can actually use it, and checking whether it can be built and sustained.

## Further reading

- [**How the discovery phase works**](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-discovery-phase-works) - Explains discovery's aim of understanding the problem before committing to build anything.
- [**Prototyping**](https://guides.18f.org/methods/make/prototyping/) - Create, observe, and discard rudimentary solutions to test a concept's viability.
- [**Making prototypes**](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/making-prototypes) - Select an appropriate prototype and avoid treating prototype code as production code.
- [**Storyboard**](https://www.designkit.org/methods/storyboard.html) - Uses a low-resolution narrative to reveal how, where, and by whom a service concept gets used.
- [**Determine What to Prototype**](https://www.designkit.org/methods/determine-what-to-prototype.html) - Isolates the critical unknowns, learning goals, and concept components that a prototype must test.
- [**Stakeholder and user interviews**](https://guides.18f.org/methods/discover/stakeholder-and-user-interviews/) - Gives a semi-structured interview method for understanding roles, needs, assumptions, and success measures.
- [**Design hypothesis**](https://guides.18f.org/methods/decide/design-hypothesis/) - Links a proposed change to an expected outcome, measurable signal, experiment, and potential harm.
